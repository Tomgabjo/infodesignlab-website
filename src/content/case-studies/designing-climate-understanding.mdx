## What was done

As the world moves closer to exceeding 1.5°C of global warming, policymakers face a new and urgent challenge: understanding the implications of surpassing this threshold and the potential pathways for returning again below it. This concept known as temperature overshoot is becoming of increasing interest in climate policy discussions, yet it remains under-communicated, inconsistently defined, and poorly understood, even among experts.

The lack of shared mental models around the term “overshoot” prompted a collaboration between an international team of climate scientists and design experts, many involved in IPCC assessments, to co-create a visual communication toolkit clarifying its meaning and implications. The work was developed in the context of the 2025 Annual Review article, _Overshoot: A Conceptual Review of Exceeding and Returning to Global Warming of 1.5°C_. The aim is to help inform a shared understanding and a more coherent dialogue, particularly in the policy-making context, with an overview of the multiple facets of this concept.

The review is the first comprehensive conceptual review exploring the implications of such “overshoot” pathways. The review considers climate-related damages and risks, adaptation and vulnerability, global emission requirements, and issues arising for global and national climate policy. The review sets out evidence and knowledge gaps that illustrate the choices and challenges that decision-makers are now facing.

## How it was done

At the heart of the review is data visualisation, not as a surface-level tool, but as a participatory process integrated early and continuously throughout the scientific workflow, unlocking the power to influence the content itself through facilitation, structured dialogues, and the evolution of visual forms. These become critical milestones for viewing the content from different perspectives, through channels that go beyond the written word. The methodology builds on the co-design principles described by Sanders and Stappers (2008), in which scientists, designers, and potential users are engaged as equal participants in the creation of communication interventions.

The work was conducted through iterative co-design cycles, each including exploratory workshops, design reviews, stakeholders’ engagement, and feedback loops. Scientists played a continuous role in identifying key messages, refining terminology, and ensuring conceptual coherence, all the while stimulated by interrogation on intent, meaning and perception by information designers. The co-design process was not an afterthought; it shaped the framing of the article itself, catalysing and informing both the narrative structure and the key scientific messages.

## By whom

Building on prior collaborations within the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) process, the information designers at InfoDesignLab worked with a multidisciplinary group of internationally recognised climate scientists, a team of high-profile experts from top institutions around the world. Several authors served or are serving as vice-chairs of one of the IPCC’s Working Groups, while others served as authors of a range of IPCC reports. CMCC’s Anna Pirani, one of the paper's authors, also has an extensive background in the IPCC, including being the former Head of the Working Group I Technical Support Unit during AR6 and now the alternate IPCC Focal Point for Italy. The team includes contributors from prestigious institutions such as CICERO in Norway, the German Institute for International and Security Affairs, the Met Office in the UK, the University of Bristol, the University of Fiji, the Institute for Small Islands, the Alfred Wegener Institute for Polar and Marine Research, the Mountain Research Initiative, the University of Bern, the Global Systems Institute at the University of Exeter, and ETH Zurich.

The co-design process of the visual package has been led by Angela Morelli and Tom Gabriel Johansen of InfoDesignLab, award-winning designers known internationally for their work at the intersection of climate science and information design.

## What conclusions were drawn

While the topic is overshoot, the core contribution of this design work lies in the value of co-design in scientific communication. This is of central importance, given that overshoot is a complex and still not widely known, or understood, concept. In many settings, the design and presentation of scientific findings is still treated as a downstream activity, commissioned at the end of a research process. A co-design approach challenges that paradigm by embedding information design from the outset and by structuring collaboration through clearly defined roles, milestones, and iterative feedback. It is a model that moves beyond the traditional “designer as translator” role to a shared creative process in which intent, stakeholders’ needs, and scientific accuracy are iteratively intertwined in real time.

This work underscores the importance of embedding design-led methodologies into the design, communication and dissemination of scientific findings relevant to decision-making at the interface of science and policy. Crucially, this is not a task to be squeezed in if time allows; it is a specialised professional practice that requires leadership from both scientific and design organisations, as well as intentional planning and early integration in order to be prioritised. Competing pressures can at times be challenging, requiring patience, guidance and facilitation on all sides but the value of investing the time and commitment is clear: it results in communication tools that can better fulfil the needs of both the audience and the scientists. The learning journey can generate a wider impact within the scientific community, equipping scientists and institutions with new mindsets and tools for presenting complex data.

By professionalising design practice within science-policy spaces, we can equip decision-makers not only with better visuals, but with a deeper and more actionable understanding of climate risk. In a world where polarisation and misinformation threaten coordinated action, the role of information design must evolve. Information designers are not only makers of artefacts, they are facilitators of understanding, translators of complexity, and co-creators of meaning. When given the tools, space, and leadership to collaborate meaningfully with scientists and stakeholders, they can help shape a global response to the important challenges the world is facing, grounded in clarity, trust, and informed decision-making.
